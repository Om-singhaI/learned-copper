# Learned Copper: Guiding PCB Autorouting with a Model Trained on Human Routed Boards

CS57600 Machine Learning, Fall 2026. Midway Report. Om Singhal (working alone).

## Progress in one paragraph

The project proposed in September has a working data pipeline, a frozen test set, a trained first model and baseline numbers. The data side took longer than planned, because the public board collections turned out to need more cleaning than any paper admits, but it produced something the field does not have: a pool of real human routed boards with honest, repository grouped splits and a fixed test set that matches a published benchmark. The first machine learning results are in: a UNet that predicts where a net's copper belongs, scored against two baselines on held out boards, under both a leaky random split and the correct grouped split. The router guidance experiments, which turn the prediction into a routing result, are the remaining work.

## Data: what was built

The proposal named open-schematics as the primary source. After measuring it (87,931 records, about 55,000 board files, 317 GB, no splits, boards repeated across schematic sheets), I adopted a better starting point found during a dataset search: the PCBench corpus of 1,157 human routed KiCad boards as mirrored by the Freerouting project, which ships each board with an unrouted twin and ground truth counts, plus PCBWorld's fixed test lists (99 easy, 10 medium, 10 hard) drawn from the same corpus with published baselines in exactly the metrics this project uses. The 119 test boards and the 94 source repositories behind them are excluded from all training; the lists are frozen as hash files in the repository and regenerated by a script.

Every board was inventoried with KiCad's own Python API (all 1,157 load; counts agree with the ground truth on every board) and checked with KiCad's design rule checker. The checker produced a finding worth reporting on its own: human boards appeared to carry thousands of clearance violations, 940 of 1,157 boards affected, until I noticed that 96 percent of those involved copper zones. Old files carry stale zone fills. Refilling zones before the check reduced a 30 board pilot from 4,304 clearance errors to 97. The filter keeps boards that are fully connected after refill, have a valid outline and tracks, no shorts or crossing tracks, and no clearance errors under the board's own rules (a flagged tier allows up to 10). Result: 633 clean boards and 68 flagged, 456 rejected, giving 530 training boards from the mirror. 22 of the 119 test boards fail this stricter filter, mostly over connectivity after refill, so the primary test set is the 97 that pass and all 119 are reported with completion measured against the human reference's own connectivity.

## Method

Rather than predicting all copper on a board at once, each training sample is one net. The input is a six channel raster at 256 by 256 pixels, scaled per board (median 0.23 mm per pixel): the board outline, this net's pads on the front and back copper layers, every other pad on each layer, and the copper of every other net. The target is this net's tracks and vias on the front and back layers. This is how a sequential router consumes guidance, it matches how prior generative routing work in chip design framed the problem, and it turns 645 two layer boards into 17,718 training samples. Copper is sparse: about 0.07 percent of pixels in a sample, which drives every modelling choice below.

Three models are compared on the same samples and splits.

* A distance heuristic: the probability of copper decays with distance from the net's own pads, with the decay length chosen on the training set. This is what a plain shortest path router effectively assumes.
* Gradient boosting (LightGBM) on per pixel features: distances to this net's pads, to other pads and to other copper on each layer, the outline flag and position. Thresholds are tuned on training boards.
* A UNet with 2.1 million parameters, four resolution levels, trained with binary cross entropy plus a Dice term for the sparse target, AdamW with a cosine schedule, rotation and flip augmentation.

Metrics are intersection over union, F1, precision and recall on copper pixels, accumulated over the whole validation or test set. The central comparison is between two split protocols. The random split pools samples and divides them at random, which lets near duplicate boards and other nets of the same board leak between train and validation. The owner split keeps every board of a source repository on one side. Both are reported so that the inflation from leakage is measured rather than assumed. Training ran on the laptop's GPU at 128 pixel resolution for the midway numbers; the 256 pixel runs follow on free cloud GPUs.

## Changes from the proposal

* Data source: the PCBench mirror and PCBWorld's test lists replace sampling open-schematics by hand; open-schematics becomes the scale up lane on Kaggle for the final weeks if time allows.
* Formulation: one sample per net instead of one per board, with other nets' copper as input context.
* Evaluation: DRC runs with zones refilled, counts only copper rule types, and the primary test set is the 97 clean test boards.
* Scope: two layer boards only (1,037 of the 1,157 in the pool), stated as the regime of every claim.
* Router guidance: the prediction will drive a grid maze router written for the project, since neither Freerouting nor PCBWorld accepts an external cost map. Net attribute channels (net class width, power versus signal, differential pairs) are planned for the model, and a net ordering ranker is the stretch goal.

## Plan to the final report

* Weeks 11 to 12: 256 pixel training on cloud GPUs; net attribute channels; ablation with and without them.
* Weeks 12 to 13: the maze router with the prediction as a per cell cost; Freerouting baseline on the 97 clean test boards under the same KiCad 10 check; completion, wire length, via count and design rule violations, guided versus unguided.
* Week 14: random versus owner split comparison on the routing metrics; error analysis by net kind and board size.
* Weeks 15 to 16: presentation and final report; release of the dataset splits, model and a demo if the numbers justify it.

## Risks

The model may predict plausible copper that a router cannot realise; the guidance experiment is the only test that matters, and a tie with the unguided router would still be reported as the finding. Compute is the laptop and free cloud sessions, so the 256 pixel runs are capped in epochs. And the regime is two layer hobby boards; nothing here is claimed for multilayer or high speed designs.
